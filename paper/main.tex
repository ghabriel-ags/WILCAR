\begin{abstract}
The probability of the positive class is often known to increase or decrease with some inputs. We study
single-hidden-layer networks grown one hidden unit at a time under such \emph{partial} monotonicity requirements and
ask for models that are monotone on the whole input domain, not only on the training data. The gains
$\partial p/\partial x_i$ and their weight gradients are written in closed form and imposed as constraints of a
sequential quadratic program at every constructive step. We show that the average-gain constraint of earlier work
does not imply monotonicity; that sign constraints on the weights, which do, fail on simple monotone targets with one
hidden layer; and that single-hidden-layer networks monotone on the domain are universal approximators of
continuous partially monotone functions. To find them, point-wise constraints are imposed at anchors that grow with
the violations found by an adversarial search, and the result is checked by a sound branch-and-bound certificate based
on interval global optimisation, exact in pre-activation space; the returned network is always certified. Model size
is selected by dynamic cross-validation. On synthetic targets with a known probability, certified networks are as
accurate as unconstrained ones and markedly better than sign-constrained ones. On eight datasets, including the COMPAS,
Heart Disease and Loan Defaulter benchmarks, under nested cross-validation against sixteen methods including Min-Max,
Constrained Monotonic and Lipschitz Monotonic networks, \todo{one-sentence result after the campaign}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In many classification problems the direction of the effect of some inputs is known before any data are seen: the
probability of death after heart failure should not decrease as the ejection fraction falls, the probability that a
tumour is malignant should not decrease as its radius grows, the probability of default should not decrease as the
debt-to-income ratio increases. A model that violates such relations may still score well on a test set, but its
predictions cannot be defended to a clinician, a regulator or a customer, and its violations tend to appear exactly
where data are scarce \citep{Sill1997,Cano2019,Liu2020}. Encoding them is a form of informed machine learning
\citep{vonRueden2023}: \emph{monotonic} networks enforce the relations, and \emph{partially} monotonic networks enforce
them only for the inputs whose direction is known \citep{DanielsVelikova2010,Minin2010,You2017}.

Existing approaches fall into three families. \emph{Soft} approaches penalise violations at sampled points
\citep{SillAbuMostafa1996} and give no guarantee outside the sample. \emph{Structural} approaches make monotonicity a
property of the parametrisation: non-negative weights \citep{ArcherWang1993,DanielsVelikova2010}, min-max networks
\citep{Sill1997}, lattices \citep{Gupta2016,You2017}, integral networks \citep{Wehenkel2019}, constrained activations
\citep{Runje2023}, isotonic embeddings \citep{Zhao2024dien}, Lipschitz-bounded residual networks
\citep{Nolte2023,Kitouni2023} or spline-based Kolmogorov--Arnold networks \citep{PoloMolina2026kan}; they are monotone
by construction but fix a specialised topology in advance. \emph{Certified} approaches train an ordinary network and
verify it, with mixed-integer programming \citep{Liu2020}, SMT solvers \citep{Sivaraman2020} or Lipschitz-based
covering of the domain \citep{PoloMolina2026cert}.

\emph{Constructive} networks \citep{Ash1989,Fahlman1990,KwokYeung1997,Parekh2000,MaKhorasani2004,HuangChenSiew2006,WangLi2017}
answer a different question --- how many hidden units --- by growing the hidden layer until a validation criterion stops
improving. Combining growth with monotonicity is not immediate. The natural structural choice for a single hidden layer,
non-negative (sign-constrained) weights, is not universal for monotone functions \citep{DanielsVelikova2010,Gupta2016};
in \cref{sec:expressive} its error on a simple two-input target stops decreasing after a few units. Constraints
imposed only on the training data, on the other hand, may hold at every size on the data and fail elsewhere. In earlier
work we imposed \emph{gain-sign} constraints --- on the sign of $\partial\hat y/\partial x_i$ --- on constructive
networks for regression, through the sign of the average finite-difference gain \citep{Sa2026}; that constraint does not
imply monotonicity either.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Expressiveness.} Single-hidden-layer sigmoid networks that are monotone on the input box are universal
approximators of continuous partially monotone functions, with a strictly positive gain margin
(\cref{thm:ua}). The obstruction to universality in one hidden layer is therefore the sign parametrisation, not the
depth; the certified class escapes it (\cref{fig:surfaces}).
\item \emph{A training procedure whose output is always certified} (\cref{sec:cegis}). Closed-form gains and their
weight gradients (\cref{sec:gains}) are imposed as point-wise constraints of a sequential quadratic program at anchors
that grow with the counterexamples found by an adversarial search; the final network is verified, repaired with the
counterexamples of the verifier, and, if verification still fails, replaced by the sign-constrained solution at the same
size (\cref{prop:always}).
\item \emph{A sound certificate specialised to single hidden layers} (\cref{sec:certificate}). The sign of a gain is the
sign of a combination of $\sig'$ terms; we bound it with interval global optimisation --- natural extension, mean-value
form, gradient enclosure and monotonicity test --- in input space, and exactly in pre-activation space, where the
combination is separable and a linear program discards unreachable regions (\cref{thm:sound,prop:term}).
\item \emph{Dynamic cross-validation} (\cref{sec:dcv}) grows one model per inner fold in lock-step and selects the size
by the aggregated validation loss, inside an outer loop never used for selection.
\item \emph{A controlled evaluation} (\cref{sec:setup}): synthetic targets with a known probability, eight real datasets
including the three classification benchmarks of the monotonic-network literature, nested cross-validation, sixteen
methods, certification rates in addition to accuracy, and ablations that separate the effect of the constraints from
that of the optimiser --- one of them shows that the advantage of weight reuse over random re-initialisation reported
in our earlier constructive work is largely an optimiser effect.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{sec:related} reviews related work, \cref{sec:problem} fixes notation, \cref{sec:method} presents the method and
its theory, \cref{sec:setup} the experimental protocol, \cref{sec:results} the results, \cref{sec:discussion} discusses
them and \cref{sec:conclusion} concludes. Proofs are in \ref{app:proofs}.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Monotonic networks}
\citet{ArcherWang1993} constrained back-propagation weights to be non-negative for two-group classification;
\citet{SillAbuMostafa1996} penalised violations at sampled pairs of points (monotonicity hints); \citet{Sill1997}
proposed min-max networks, monotone by construction and universal for monotone functions. \citet{DanielsVelikova2010}
extended min-max networks to partial monotonicity and clarified the limits of non-negative weights: one hidden layer
does not suffice to approximate every monotone function \citep[see also][]{Gupta2016}; \citet{Minin2010} compared such
structural approximators. For threshold units, \citet{Mikulincer2025} showed that monotone networks with one hidden
layer cannot even interpolate every monotone data set, while four layers suffice for approximation. Later work removed
the expressiveness limitations with lattices \citep{Gupta2016,You2017}, integrals of positive networks
\citep{Wehenkel2019}, mixtures of convex, concave and saturated activations \citep{Runje2023}, a Lipschitz-bounded
network plus a monotone linear term \citep{Nolte2023,Kitouni2023}, separate monotone and free embeddings
\citep{Zhao2024dien,KimLee2024} and splines \citep{PoloMolina2026kan}. For extreme learning machines, \citet{Zhu2017}
solved a quadratic program on the output weights of a fixed random layer; ordinal and monotonic classification more
broadly is surveyed by \citet{Gutierrez2016} and \citet{Cano2019}. All of these fix the architecture in advance.

\subsection{Certified monotonicity and verification}
\citet{Liu2020} train ReLU networks with a monotonicity regulariser and certify them by mixed-integer linear
programming, increasing the penalty until certification succeeds; \citet{Sivaraman2020} (COMET) find counterexamples
with an SMT solver, augment the training data with them and compute monotone envelopes at prediction time;
\citet{PoloMolina2026cert} certify the positivity of partial derivatives of a trained network by covering the domain with
balls whose radius follows from a Lipschitz bound of the derivative. Our certificate belongs to the same family but
exploits the structure of a single hidden layer: the sign of a gain is a combination of $\sig'$ terms, which we bound
with the tools of interval global optimisation \citep{HansenWalster2004,Moore2009} and, in pre-activation space, exactly.
Our training loop follows counterexample-guided inductive synthesis \citep{SolarLezama2006}: the learner satisfies the
constraints on a finite set, and a verifier either proves them everywhere or returns a point that enters the set. Unlike
COMET, counterexamples become hard constraints of the optimisation rather than additional training examples, and
unlike \citet{Liu2020} no penalty weight has to be tuned. Neural network verification in general --- bound propagation,
branch and bound, MILP and SMT --- is surveyed by \citet{Liu2021verif}; see also \citet{Katz2017,Tjeng2019,Gowal2019,Zhao2024verif}.

\subsection{Constructive networks and model selection}
Constructive algorithms add hidden units one at a time \citep{Ash1989,Fahlman1990,Platt1991,KwokYeung1997}, freezing
the existing units \citep{Fahlman1990}, retraining all weights \citep{Lehtokangas1999,MaKhorasani2004}, or drawing
random units whose output weights are solved in closed form \citep{HuangChenSiew2006}; stochastic configuration networks
add random units subject to a supervisory inequality \citep{WangLi2017,Huang2021scn,Nan2024}. Constructive classifiers
were reviewed by \citet{Parekh2000}, and quasi-Newton training inside a construction algorithm was proposed by
\citet{SetionoHui1995}. Weight initialisation by linearisation around the optimal linear model, combined with growth and
weight reuse (WILCAR), was proposed for regression and multiclass classification \citep{Sa2022,Fontes2021}. Model size
has been selected with a static hold-out set \citep{Setiono2001}, with cross-validation over candidate architectures
\citep{Aran2009} and with early-stopping criteria \citep{Prechelt1998nn}. Selecting hyper-parameters on the data used to
report performance biases the estimate \citep{VarmaSimon2006,CawleyTalbot2010}, and warm starting from previously trained
weights can hurt generalisation \citep{AshAdams2020}, which makes weight reuse in constructive networks a question to
test rather than assume. None of these works considers monotonicity.

\section{Problem setting}\label{sec:problem}

Let $x\in\R^p$ be scaled by min-max normalisation fitted on the training data, so that the training inputs lie in the
box $\B=[0,1]^p$, and let $y\in\{0,1\}$. A single-hidden-layer feedforward network (SLFN) with $n$ sigmoid units models
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slfn}
p_\theta(x)=\sig\bigl(z_o(x)\bigr),\qquad z_o(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n} v_j\,\sig\bigl(z_j(x)\bigr)+c,\qquad z_j(x)=w_j^\top x+b_j ,
\end{equation}
with $\theta=(W,b,v,c)$, $W\in\R^{n\times p}$ with rows $w_j^\top$, and $\sig(t)=1/(1+\exp(-t))$; $p_\theta(x)$
estimates $P(Y=1\mid x)$ and the class is assigned at threshold $0.5$. SLFNs are universal approximators
\citep{Hornik1989,Leshno1993}, also of derivatives \citep{Hornik1991}.

Prior knowledge is encoded in a \emph{signal vector} $s\in\{-1,0,+1\}^p$: $s_i=+1$ ($-1$) states that $P(Y=1\mid x)$
does not decrease (increase) with $x_i$ when the other inputs are fixed, and $s_i=0$ leaves $x_i$ unconstrained. Let
$\I=\{i: s_i\neq0\}$.

\begin{definition}[Partial monotonicity]\label{def:mono}
A function $f:\B\to\R$ is \emph{$s$-monotone on $\B$} if, for every $i\in\I$, $x\in\B$ and $t\ge0$ with $x+te_i\in\B$,
$s_i\bigl(f(x+te_i)-f(x)\bigr)\ge0$. For the smooth $p_\theta$ this holds if and only if the \emph{gains}
$g_i(x;\theta)=\partial p_\theta(x)/\partial x_i$ satisfy $s_i\,g_i(x;\theta)\ge0$ for all $x\in\B$ and $i\in\I$.
\end{definition}

The guarantee is stated on $\B$, the region covered by the training data after scaling; test points outside it are
extrapolations, for which no data-driven model can be certified without further assumptions.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Constructive networks}\label{sec:constructive}

A constructive method trains a sequence of networks $\theta_1,\theta_2,\dots$ with $n=1,2,\dots$ hidden units, stops
when a validation criterion has not improved for a number of steps (the \emph{patience}) and returns the best size
$n^\ast$. We study three members of this family and their constrained variants. \emph{WILCAR} \citep{Sa2022,Fontes2021}
initialises the first unit by linearising the network around the mean input so that it matches the least-squares linear
model, and \emph{reuses weights}: the network with $n$ units starts from the trained network with $n-1$ units plus one
new unit, so that successive networks form a continuous path. \emph{RIXM} re-initialises all weights at every size from
a zero-mean Gaussian with variance equal to the inverse fan-in, a scaled initialisation of the Xavier family
\citep{Glorot2010}. \emph{ELM} \citep{Huang2006} draws the hidden layer at random (ReLU units,
weights and biases uniform in $[-1,1]$) and solves the output layer by least squares. The constrained variants are
WILCAR-C, RIXM-C and ELM-C.

WILCAR and RIXM minimise the regularised cross-entropy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
J(\theta)=-\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl[y_k\log p_\theta(x_k)+(1-y_k)\log\bigl(1-p_\theta(x_k)\bigr)\bigr]
+\frac{\lambda_0}{N}\bigl(\|W\|_F^2+\|v\|_2^2\bigr),
\end{equation}
the negative log-posterior per sample under a Gaussian prior of precision $\lambda_0$ on the weights. Unconstrained
networks are trained with L-BFGS \citep{LiuNocedal1989}; constrained ones start from that unconstrained optimum and are
then solved with SLSQP \citep{Kraft1994}, a sequential quadratic programming method \citep{NocedalWright2006}. Both are
quasi-Newton methods using the analytic gradient of \eqref{eq:loss}, so that differences between a method and its
constrained variant are due to the constraints, not to the optimiser (\cref{sec:ablation}). The logistic output of ELM-C
is fitted by SLSQP on the output weights, with the hidden layer fixed.

\subsection{Closed-form gains}\label{sec:gains}

\begin{lemma}[Gains]\label{lem:gain}
For the network \eqref{eq:slfn},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gain}
g_i(x;\theta)=\sig'\bigl(z_o(x)\bigr)\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_j\,\sig'\bigl(z_j(x)\bigr)\,W_{ji},
\qquad \sig'(t)=\sig(t)\bigl(1-\sig(t)\bigr),
\end{equation}
and, with $c_{ij}=s_i\,v_j\,W_{ji}$ and $h_i(x;\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\,\sig'(z_j(x))$,
$s_i\,g_i(x;\theta)=\sig'(z_o(x))\,h_i(x;\theta)$. Since $\sig'>0$, $s_i g_i(x;\theta)\ge0 \iff h_i(x;\theta)\ge0$.
\end{lemma}

The derivatives of $g_i$ with respect to every weight follow by differentiating \eqref{eq:gain} and are given in
\ref{app:gradients}; SLSQP therefore receives exact constraint Jacobians instead of finite differences. The quantity
$h_i$ carries the whole sign information and is used by the certificate.

\subsection{Constraint families}\label{sec:constraints}

At every constructive step the constrained methods solve $\min_\theta J(\theta)$ subject to one of the following sets of
constraints, where $\A\subset\B$ is a finite set of \emph{anchors}:
\begin{align}
\text{mean:}\quad & \frac{1}{|\A|}\sum_{a\in\A}s_i\,g_i(a;\theta)\ \ge\ \varepsilon_{m}, && i\in\I, \label{eq:mean}\\
\text{anchor:}\quad & s_i\,g_i(a;\theta)\ \ge\ \varepsilon_{p}, && i\in\I,\ a\in\A, \label{eq:point}\\
\text{sign:}\quad & c_{ij}=s_i\,v_j\,W_{ji}\ \ge\ 0, && i\in\I,\ j=1,\dots,n. \label{eq:sign}
\end{align}
The mean constraint \eqref{eq:mean}, with $\A$ the training inputs (a random subset of 1000 of them when there are more),
is the constraint of our earlier work \citep{Sa2026}, with the finite-difference gain replaced by the exact one. It does not imply monotonicity: an average of $g_i$ can be positive while $g_i$ is negative on a region
of positive measure, and a finite-sample average says nothing about $\B\setminus\A$. The anchor constraint
\eqref{eq:point}, with $k$-means centres as anchors, is stronger but still covers only $\A$. The sign constraint is
sufficient everywhere:

\begin{proposition}[Sign constraints]\label{prop:sign}
If \eqref{eq:sign} holds, $p_\theta$ is $s$-monotone on $\R^p$.
\end{proposition}

\Cref{prop:sign} is the non-negative-weight construction \citep{ArcherWang1993,DanielsVelikova2010} written for partial
monotonicity: flipping the sign of each decreasing input and of each unit with $v_j<0$ (absorbing the constant into $c$)
turns \eqref{eq:sign} into non-negative weights on the constrained paths. For ELM-C, whose hidden layer is fixed,
\eqref{eq:sign} is linear in the output weights; we draw the hidden weights of constrained inputs with the prescribed
sign, so that \eqref{eq:sign} reduces to non-negative output weights (a monotone ELM). If SLSQP ends at an infeasible
point, the step is restarted from a random initialisation, up to $R$ times; a size at which no feasible network is found
counts as a non-improving step.

\subsection{Expressiveness}\label{sec:expressive}

The sign constraint pays for its guarantee with expressiveness: every hidden unit must be monotone, with the prescribed
direction, in every constrained input. A single hidden layer of such units cannot approximate every monotone function
\citep{DanielsVelikova2010,Gupta2016}. \Cref{fig:surfaces} shows a concrete case: the target
$P(Y=1\mid x)=\sig\bigl(12(\min\{x_1,x_2\}-0.45)\bigr)$, increasing in both inputs, has an L-shaped decision boundary
with a sharp corner at $(0.45,0.45)$ (panel a). Under the sign constraint every hidden unit is a sigmoid ridge that
increases in both inputs, so the logit is a sum of such ridges and its level sets bend smoothly: the fitted boundary is
a rounded hyperbola that cuts the corner (panel c), and the error is three times that of the unconstrained network.
Panel (e) shows why: each arrow is a hidden unit, drawn as $v_jw_j$, the direction and strength with which it moves the
logit; the sign constraint confines every arrow to the grey quadrant, and the network in fact relies on essentially two
units, one per input, i.e.\ an additive model. The unconstrained network (panel b) forms the corner, but in two regions --- a
band in the upper left, where $P\approx0$, and a small area on the right --- increasing an input lowers the
probability; the violations are small in size, sit where the data give little information, and cannot be seen in the
accuracy, which is why they need a certificate to be ruled out. The cegis network (panels d and f) forms the corner and
is certified: two of its units point out of the quadrant, one in the direction of $x_2-x_1$ and one of $x_1-x_2$, so
that each on its own is decreasing in one input; together they act like the term $-|x_1-x_2|$ in
$\min\{x_1,x_2\}=\tfrac12(x_1+x_2-|x_1-x_2|)$, which is what sharpens the corner, while the other units keep every gain
positive. \Cref{fig:capacity} repeats the comparison over the number of hidden units: with the sign constraint the
error stops decreasing after two units on both targets, whereas the cegis networks follow the unconstrained ones and
remain certified. The obstruction lies in the parametrisation, not in the single hidden layer:

\begin{theorem}[Universal approximation with a certificate margin]\label{thm:ua}
Let $f:\B\to(0,1)$ be continuous and $s$-monotone on $\B$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an SLFN \eqref{eq:slfn}
with $\sup_{x\in\B}|p_\theta(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon$ and $\min_{x\in\B}h_i(x;\theta)>0$ for every $i\in\I$; in particular
$p_\theta$ is $s$-monotone on $\B$ and \cref{alg:bb} certifies it after finitely many boxes (\cref{prop:term}).
\end{theorem}

The proof (\ref{app:proofs}) smooths $f$ by a monotone mollification, adds a small linear term that creates a strictly
positive gain margin, and uses the density of SLFNs in $C^1$ \citep{Hornik1991}: an approximation of the logit and of
its first derivatives preserves the margin. The argument is elementary once $C^1$ density is available; its point is
that, for a single hidden layer, the failure of the sign parametrisation is not a limitation of the architecture:
networks that are monotone on the domain are as expressive as unconstrained ones, and the margin makes the certificate
complete for them. The remaining question is how to find them.

\subsection{Counterexample-guided constraints}\label{sec:cegis}

The \emph{cegis} mode searches the certified class of \cref{thm:ua} directly (\cref{alg:cegis}). At size $n$, the network
is first trained without constraints (L-BFGS from the method's usual initialisation), then projected by SLSQP onto the
anchor constraints \eqref{eq:point}; starting from the unconstrained optimum avoids the poor local minima that SLSQP
reaches from a random, infeasible start. An adversarial search then minimises $h_i(\,\cdot\,;\theta)$ over $\B$ for each
$i\in\I$ with a bound-constrained quasi-Newton method \citep{Byrd1995} from several starting points (random points and
the training points with the smallest $h_i$), using $\nabla_x h_i(x)=\sum_j c_{ij}\,\sig''(z_j(x))\,w_j$. Every point
with $h_i<0$ becomes an anchor and the network is re-solved from its current weights. The anchors found at size $n$
are inherited at size $n+1$; with weight reuse (WILCAR-C) the network they constrain is also inherited.

After the final refit, \cref{alg:bb} is run; its counterexamples are added to the anchors and the network re-solved
(\emph{repair}). If the network is still not certified after $R_{\mathrm{rep}}$ repairs, it is projected onto the sign
constraints --- the weights $W_{ji}$ with $c_{ij}<0$ are set to zero --- and re-solved by SLSQP under \eqref{eq:sign}
from that point; any product that remains negative at the solver tolerance is set to zero again, so that \eqref{eq:sign}
holds exactly (\emph{fallback}).

\begin{proposition}[Certified output]\label{prop:always}
The network returned by the cegis mode is $s$-monotone on $\B$.
\end{proposition}

Either \cref{alg:bb} certified it (\cref{thm:sound}) or it satisfies \eqref{eq:sign} exactly (\cref{prop:sign}). We
report how often the fallback is used; in the experiments it measures how much of the flexibility of \cref{thm:ua} the
procedure actually reaches.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Constructive step $n$ in the cegis mode.}\label{alg:cegis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $(X,y)$, signal vector $s$, anchors $\A$ ($k$-means centres plus counterexamples found at earlier sizes),
initial weights $\theta^{(0)}$ (WILCAR: previous network plus one unit; RIXM: random)
\State $\theta\gets$ L-BFGS on $J$ from $\theta^{(0)}$ \Comment{unconstrained optimum}
\State $\theta\gets$ SLSQP on $J$ subject to \eqref{eq:point} on $\A$, from $\theta$; random restarts while infeasible (at most $R$)
\For{$r=1,\dots,R_{\mathrm{cex}}$}
  \State $C\gets\emptyset$
  \For{$i\in\I$}
    \State $\hat x_i\gets$ multi-start L-BFGS-B on $\min_{x\in\B}h_i(x;\theta)$
    \If{$h_i(\hat x_i;\theta)<0$} $C\gets C\cup\{\hat x_i\}$ \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{$C=\emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State $\A\gets\A\cup C$; \ $\theta\gets$ SLSQP on the enlarged $\A$, warm-started at $\theta$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\theta$, $\A$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{A sound certificate}\label{sec:certificate}

By \cref{lem:gain}, $p_\theta$ is $s$-monotone on $\B$ if and only if $\min_{x\in\B}h_i(x;\theta)\ge0$ for every
$i\in\I$. \Cref{alg:bb} decides this by branch and bound, for one $i$ at a time, with $c_j=c_{ij}$ and
$h(x)=\sum_j c_j\sig'(w_j^\top x+b_j)$. If $c_j\ge0$ for every $j$, the root is certified immediately
(\cref{prop:sign}). The bounds rely on elementary facts: $\sig'$ is even, increasing on $(-\infty,0]$ and decreasing
on $[0,\infty)$; $\sig''$ is odd, with its maximum $\sqrt3/18$ at $-t^\ast$ and its minimum $-\sqrt3/18$ at $t^\ast$,
$t^\ast=\ln(2+\sqrt3)$, and monotone between these points and on either side of them.

\paragraph{Input-space bounds}
For a box $Q=[l,u]\subseteq\B$ with centre $m$ and radius $r=(u-l)/2$, each pre-activation ranges exactly over
$[\alpha_j,\beta_j]=[w_j^\top m+b_j-|w_j|^\top r,\ w_j^\top m+b_j+|w_j|^\top r]$. With
$\underline{d}_j=\min\{\sig'(\alpha_j),\sig'(\beta_j)\}$, $\overline{d}_j=\sig'(\clip(0;\alpha_j,\beta_j))$, and
$[\underline{e}_j,\overline{e}_j]$ the range of $\sig''$ on $[\alpha_j,\beta_j]$, the partial derivatives
$\partial h/\partial x_d=\sum_j c_jW_{jd}\sig''(z_j)$ are enclosed on $Q$ by intervals $[G^-_d,G^+_d]$ obtained by
interval arithmetic, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lbx}
L(Q)=\max\Bigl\{\sum_{c_j\ge0}c_j\underline{d}_j+\sum_{c_j<0}c_j\overline{d}_j,\ \ h(m)-\sum_{d=1}^{p}r_d\max\{|G^-_d|,|G^+_d|\}\Bigr\}
\le\min_{x\in Q}h(x).
\end{equation}
The first term is the natural interval extension, the second the mean-value form, which becomes tight as boxes shrink
\citep{Moore2009}. Before bounding, the \emph{monotonicity test} of interval global optimisation
\citep{HansenWalster2004} collapses the box: if $G^-_d>0$ the minimum of $h$ on $Q$ lies on the face $x_d=l_d$, and if
$G^+_d<0$ on the face $x_d=u_d$.

\paragraph{Pre-activation-space bounds}
When $n<p$, $h=\varphi\circ(Wx+b)$ with $\varphi(z)=\sum_j c_j\sig'(z_j)$ \emph{separable}. On a box
$R=[\zeta^l,\zeta^u]\subset\R^n$ its minimum is therefore exact:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lbz}
\min_{z\in R}\varphi(z)=\sum_{c_j\ge0}c_j\min\{\sig'(\zeta^l_j),\sig'(\zeta^u_j)\}+\sum_{c_j<0}c_j\,\sig'\bigl(\clip(0;\zeta^l_j,\zeta^u_j)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The pre-activations reachable from $\B$ form the zonotope $Z=\{Wx+b:x\in\B\}$; a box with $R\cap Z=\emptyset$ is
discarded, which a linear program in $x$ decides. The search starts from the bounding box of $Z$, splits the box with
the most negative bound first and, for boxes that meet $Z$, evaluates $h$ at the solution of the linear program
(minimising the linearisation of $\varphi$ at the centre of $R$), which yields counterexamples.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Branch-and-bound certificate for input $i$ ($h=h_i$).}\label{alg:bb}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\If{$c_j\ge0$ for all $j$} \Return \textsc{certified} \EndIf
\State $\mathcal{Q}\gets\{\B\}$ \Comment{input space; pre-activation space when $n<p$ and the input-space search exhausts $N/4$ boxes}
\While{$\mathcal{Q}\neq\emptyset$}
  \State remove a box $Q$ from $\mathcal{Q}$; apply the monotonicity test to $Q$
  \If{$L(Q)\ge0$ (\eqref{eq:lbx}; \eqref{eq:lbz} in pre-activation space) \textbf{or} $Q$ does not meet $Z$} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
  \State $\hat x\gets$ centre of $Q$ (pre-activation space: point returned by the linear program)
  \If{$h(\hat x)<0$} \Return \textsc{counterexample} $\hat x$ \EndIf
  \If{more than $N$ boxes processed} \Return \textsc{unknown} \EndIf
  \State split $Q$ in half along $\argmax_d (u_d-l_d)\max\{|G^-_d|,|G^+_d|\}$ (pre-activation space: $\argmax_j(\zeta^u_j-\zeta^l_j)|c_j|$); add both halves to $\mathcal{Q}$
\EndWhile
\State \Return \textsc{certified}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[Soundness]\label{thm:sound}
If \cref{alg:bb} returns \textsc{certified} for every $i\in\I$, then $p_\theta$ is $s$-monotone on $\B$. If it returns
\textsc{counterexample} $\hat x$, then $\hat x\in\B$ and $s_i\,g_i(\hat x;\theta)<0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[Termination]\label{prop:term}
If $\min_{\B}h_i\neq0$ and boxes are processed in breadth-first order, the input-space search terminates after finitely
many boxes when $N=\infty$.
\end{proposition}

By \cref{thm:ua}, networks with a positive margin are dense, and for them the search is complete. The only undecided
case is a minimum exactly at zero, where the network is monotone but touches the boundary; the search then stops at
the budget with \textsc{unknown}, which the cegis mode resolves by the fallback. The
certificate is exact up to floating-point rounding; directed rounding \citep{Moore2009} would make it rigorous in that
sense as well. \Cref{thm:sound} holds for any SLFN, so we also certify the networks trained with the mean and anchor
constraints and the unconstrained ones, which measures how often their monotonicity holds without a guarantee.

\paragraph{Cost}
Bounding a box costs $O(np)$ operations (the pre-activation intervals, the enclosures of $\sig'$ and $\sig''$ and the
gradient enclosure), and boxes are processed in vectorised batches. In pre-activation space each box costs one linear
program with $p$ variables and $2n$ inequalities. The number of boxes is exponential in the dimension of the search
space in the worst case, as for any complete global method; the practical cost is governed by the margin
$\min_\B h_i$, since a box is discarded as soon as its radius falls below a multiple of that margin (proof of
\cref{prop:term}), and by the dimension, which is $\min\{n,p\}$. The certificate is run once per constrained input on
the final network; its time is reported in \cref{sec:cost}. The quadratic programs of SLSQP, whose cost grows with the
cube of the number of weights $n(p+2)+1$, are the dominant cost of the constrained methods.

\subsection{Dynamic cross-validation}\label{sec:dcv}

\Cref{alg:dcv} selects the number of units inside a training set. The set is split into $K$ stratified folds, one model
per fold is created, and all $K$ models are grown in lock-step; at size $n$ the criterion is the sum of the $K$ validation
cross-entropies. Growth stops when the criterion has not improved for the patience, and the final network is retrained on
the whole training set with the selected size $n^\ast$ (for WILCAR, along the constructive path $1,\dots,n^\ast$). The
criterion is informative only if the $K$ curves are smooth functions of $n$, which weight reuse provides and random
re-initialisation does not; we test this by also running DCV for RIXM (\cref{sec:ablation}). The alternative selection
is a stratified 80/20 hold-out split with the validation MCC as criterion. DCV aggregates the cross-entropy rather than
the MCC because the MCC is piecewise constant in the weights and its sum over folds changes in jumps, which makes the
patience rule erratic; the hold-out split, with a single curve, uses the classification metric directly.

Under the prior of \eqref{eq:loss} additional units rarely hurt the validation loss, so $J(n)$ is often flat beyond a
few units and its minimiser can lie far out on the plateau (\cref{sec:cost}). We therefore also apply the
one-standard-error rule \citep{Breiman1984,Hastie2009}: with $L_k(n)$ the validation loss of fold $k$, the size
$n^\ast_{1\mathrm{SE}}$ is the smallest $n$ with $J(n)\le J(n^\ast)+\sqrt K\,\mathrm{sd}_k\bigl(L_k(n^\ast)\bigr)$, the
standard error of $J$ at the minimum. Both choices come from the same run and are reported as DCV and DCV-1SE.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Dynamic cross-validation (DCV) inside a training set $T$.}\label{alg:dcv}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State split $T$ into $K$ stratified folds $(T_k^{\mathrm{tr}},T_k^{\mathrm{va}})$; initialise one model $M_k$ per fold on $T_k^{\mathrm{tr}}$
\State $J^\ast\gets\infty$, $w\gets0$
\For{$n=1,2,\dots,n_{\max}$}
  \For{$k=1,\dots,K$} \State $M_k\gets$ constructive step $n$ of $M_k$ on $T_k^{\mathrm{tr}}$ (warm start from $M_k$ at $n-1$ if the method reuses weights) \EndFor
  \State $L_k(n)\gets\mathrm{BCE}\bigl(M_k;T_k^{\mathrm{va}}\bigr)$, $J(n)\gets\sum_k L_k(n)$ \ ($J(n)=\infty$ if some $M_k$ is infeasible)
  \If{$J(n)<J^\ast$} $J^\ast\gets J(n)$, $n^\ast\gets n$, $w\gets0$ \Else\ $w\gets w+1$; \textbf{if} $w=$ patience \textbf{break} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State $n^\ast_{1\mathrm{SE}}\gets\min\{n: J(n)\le J^\ast+\sqrt K\,\mathrm{sd}_k(L_k(n^\ast))\}$ \Comment{one-standard-error rule}
\State \Return networks retrained on $T$ with $n^\ast$ (DCV) and $n^\ast_{1\mathrm{SE}}$ (DCV-1SE) units
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data}
\emph{Synthetic targets.} Seven functions $P(Y=1\mid x)$ on $[0,1]^p$, $p\in\{2,3\}$, known and $s$-monotone
(\ref{app:signals}): the ``and'' target of \cref{fig:surfaces}, its three-input version, a version with a free periodic
input, a version with a decreasing input, an ``or'' target (maximum instead of minimum), a product target and a smooth
``wave'' whose gains stay between 1.5 and 10.5 but whose natural representation uses the non-monotone ridge
$\sin\bigl(3(x_1-x_2)\bigr)$. The minimum and maximum targets favour Min-Max networks, whose functional form is a maximum
of minima of hyperplanes; the product and wave targets do not. Each
data set has 2000 uniform inputs with Bernoulli labels. Because the probability is known, the error of $p_\theta$
against it is measured on $2\times10^4$ uniform points, which isolates approximation from label noise. Since the
question here is what each constrained class can represent, every constructive step of every SLFN --- free or
constrained --- keeps the best of three initialisations by training objective; a single start often ends in a poor basin
(on the ``or'' target, an error of 0.111 instead of 0.023 for the unconstrained network), which would confound the
comparison. On the real data a single initialisation is used, as is usual.

\emph{Real data.} \Cref{tab:datasets} lists the eight datasets. The first five come from the UCI repository; their
signal vectors, derived from domain knowledge, are given in \ref{app:signals}. The last three are the classification
benchmarks of the monotonic-network literature \citep{Liu2020,Sivaraman2020,Nolte2023,Runje2023}, used with the
preprocessing, train/test split and monotone features of the public release of \citet{Runje2023}. Loan Defaulter is
subsampled (stratified) to 20\,000 rows for cross-validation, because each constrained step solves a dense quadratic
program.

\IfFileExists{tables/datasets.tex}{\input{tables/datasets.tex}}{}

\subsection{Protocol}
Performance is estimated by nested cross-validation \citep{VarmaSimon2006,CawleyTalbot2010}: an outer stratified 5-fold
split repeated five times with different seeds (twice for COMPAS and Loan Defaulter), the outer test fold used exactly
once. Inside each outer training set, inputs are scaled to $[0,1]$ with statistics of that set, the model size (or the
hyper-parameters of the baselines) is selected on a stratified 80/20 split or by DCV with $K=4$, and the final model is
refitted on the whole outer training set. All methods see the same outer and inner splits. For the three benchmarks we
additionally train on the official training set and test on the official test set with five seeds (\cref{sec:lit}),
the protocol of the published results.

\subsection{Methods and hyper-parameters}
Constructive networks: WILCAR, RIXM and ELM (unconstrained; $n_{\max}=300$, patience 30) and WILCAR-C, RIXM-C and ELM-C
($n_{\max}=50$, patience 15) with the mean, anchor, sign and cegis constraints; $\varepsilon_m=0.05$,
$\varepsilon_p=10^{-3}$, ten $k$-means anchors, $R=100$ restarts, $\lambda_0=0.3$, at most 1000 iterations of SLSQP
and L-BFGS, constraint tolerance $10^{-6}$, $R_{\mathrm{cex}}=10$ counterexample rounds with 16 starts,
$R_{\mathrm{rep}}=20$ repairs, and certificate budgets of $2\times10^5$ boxes (input space) and 3000 linear programs
(pre-activation space). Each selection loop has a budget of one hour. Baselines, with hyper-parameters chosen on the same
validation split and refitted on training plus validation data: logistic regression, free and with sign-bounded
coefficients (LR, LR-C); XGBoost \citep{ChenGuestrin2016} and LightGBM \citep{Ke2017}, free and with monotone constraints
(XGB, XGB-C, LGBM, LGBM-C); a one-hidden-layer MLP \citep{Pedregosa2011}; Min-Max networks \citep{Sill1997}; Constrained
Monotonic Neural Networks \citep{Runje2023} and Lipschitz Monotonic Networks \citep{Nolte2023}, both from their authors'
packages. The last three are monotone by construction and are trained with Adam \citep{KingmaBa2015} and early stopping.
The grids are listed in \ref{app:hyper}.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
The primary metric is the Matthews correlation coefficient \citep{Matthews1975,ChiccoJurman2020}; we also report the area
under the ROC curve and the Brier score \citep{Brier1950}, and, on synthetic data, the mean absolute error against the
true probability. Monotonicity is measured by (i) the share of (point, constrained input) pairs whose finite-difference
gain has the wrong sign, on the test points and on $10^4$ uniform points of $\B$, and (ii) the outcome of \cref{alg:bb}
on the final model. Methods are compared over datasets with the Friedman test, the Iman--Davenport correction and the
Nemenyi critical difference \citep{Demsar2006,ImanDavenport1980}, with Holm's correction \citep{Holm1979} for comparisons
with the best method, and paired within datasets with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test \citep{Wilcoxon1945} and the
corrected resampled $t$-test of \citet{NadeauBengio2003}, which accounts for the overlap between repeated
cross-validation folds.

\subsection{Implementation}
Everything is implemented in Python with NumPy, SciPy \citep{Virtanen2020} (SLSQP, L-BFGS-B, HiGHS linear programs) and
scikit-learn \citep{Pedregosa2011}; the monotone baselines use PyTorch and the packages \texttt{mononet} and
\texttt{monotonicnetworks}. In the code, the anchor and sign modes are called \texttt{multi} and \texttt{global}. The closed-form gains, their gradients and the certificate are covered by unit tests,
including soundness checks of the certificate against dense sampling. Experiments ran on a 16-thread workstation, one
task per core.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What each constraint guarantees}
Constraining the average gain over the training data is a single inequality per input, but it does not constrain the
sign of the gain at any particular point, let alone between the points; any claim of monotonicity must rest on a
guarantee over the domain. Sign constraints provide it through the parameters alone, at the price shown by
\cref{fig:surfaces} and \cref{thm:ua}: they exclude monotone functions that a single hidden layer can otherwise represent.
The cegis mode obtains the guarantee through verification and keeps the flexibility. \todo{quantify with the final
numbers.}

\paragraph{Why constructive growth helps}
Growing the network one unit at a time interacts well with counterexample-guided training: the anchors accumulated at
size $n$ remain valid constraints at size $n+1$, so with weight reuse the verification effort is amortised along the
constructive path, and the certificate is cheapest for the small networks that the selection favours, since the
pre-activation formulation works in dimension $n$. \todo{support with the counts of rounds/anchors by size.}

\paragraph{Weight reuse}
\todo{Interpret the optimiser ablation: warm starting accumulates optimisation across sizes and compensates for a weak
optimiser; with a quasi-Newton optimiser its benefit is cost, smooth selection curves (DCV) and stable constrained
growth, not accuracy. Relate to \citet{AshAdams2020}.}

\paragraph{Limitations}
The certificate is specific to single-hidden-layer networks: separability in pre-activation space does not survive a
second hidden layer, where the same scheme would need bound propagation through the layers \citep{Gowal2019,Liu2021verif}.
Branch and bound is exponential in the worst case; the pre-activation formulation reduces the dimension from $p$ to $n$,
which is small for the selected networks, and undecided cases are handled by repair and fallback rather than hidden. The
guarantee holds on the training box $\B$; categorical inputs encoded as indicators are treated as continuous, which is
conservative; and the quadratic programs limit the constrained networks to tens of units and the training sets to tens
of thousands of rows.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
Single-hidden-layer networks that are monotone on their input domain are universal approximators of continuous
partially monotone functions, while the sign constraints that guarantee monotonicity through the parameters are not.
We showed how to reach the certified class with constructive networks: closed-form gain constraints at anchors that grow
with counterexamples, a sound branch-and-bound certificate that exploits the structure of a single hidden layer, and a
repair-and-fallback step that makes every returned network certified. \todo{Summarise the empirical results.} Extending
the certificate to deeper constructive networks through bound propagation, and the approach to ordinal and multiclass
problems with monotone class orderings, are natural next steps.

\section{Proofs}\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{\Cref{lem:gain}}
By the chain rule,
\[
\frac{\partial p_\theta}{\partial x_i}=\sig'(z_o)\,\frac{\partial z_o}{\partial x_i},\qquad
\frac{\partial z_o}{\partial x_i}=\sum_j v_j\sig'(z_j)W_{ji}.
\]
Multiplying by $s_i$ gives $s_ig_i=\sig'(z_o)\sum_j s_iv_jW_{ji}\sig'(z_j)=\sig'(z_o)h_i$, and $\sig'(z_o)>0$.

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:sign}}
If $c_{ij}\ge0$ for all $j$, then $h_i(x)=\sum_jc_{ij}\sig'(z_j(x))\ge0$ for every $x\in\R^p$ because $\sig'>0$;
conclude with \cref{lem:gain} and \cref{def:mono}.

\paragraph{\Cref{thm:ua}}
Let $F=\sig^{-1}\circ f$. Since $f(\B)$ is a compact subset of $(0,1)$, $F$ is continuous and, $\sig^{-1}$ being
increasing, $s$-monotone. \emph{Extension.} Let $\pi:\R^p\to\B$ clip every coordinate to $[0,1]$ and
$\tilde F=F\circ\pi$; $\tilde F$ is continuous, bounded and $s$-monotone on $\R^p$, because increasing $x_i$ increases
$\pi(x)_i$ or leaves it unchanged and does not affect the other coordinates. \emph{Smoothing.} Let $\phi_\delta\ge0$ be a
smooth mollifier supported in the ball of radius $\delta$ with $\int\phi_\delta=1$, and $F_\delta=\tilde F*\phi_\delta$.
Then $F_\delta\in C^\infty$, and for $t\ge0$,
$s_i\bigl(F_\delta(x+te_i)-F_\delta(x)\bigr)=\int\phi_\delta(y)\,s_i\bigl(\tilde F(x-y+te_i)-\tilde F(x-y)\bigr)dy\ge0$, so
$s_i\,\partial_iF_\delta\ge0$; by uniform continuity of $\tilde F$ on a neighbourhood of $\B$,
$\sup_\B|F_\delta-F|\to0$ as $\delta\to0$. \emph{Margin.} Let $\eta>0$ and
$H=F_\delta+\eta\sum_{i\in\I}s_ix_i$; then $s_i\partial_iH\ge\eta$ on $\B$ for $i\in\I$. \emph{Approximation.} The
logistic function is non-constant and has bounded derivatives of every order, so by \citet[Theorem 3]{Hornik1991} functions of the form
$z_o(x)=\sum_jv_j\sig(w_j^\top x+b_j)+c$ are dense in $C^1$ on compact sets: there is $z_o$ with
$\sup_\B|z_o-H|<\eta$ and $\sup_\B|\partial_iz_o-\partial_iH|<\eta/2$ for $i\in\I$. Hence
$s_i\partial_iz_o>\eta/2$ on $\B$, and $p_\theta=\sig(z_o)$ has $s_ig_i=\sig'(z_o)s_i\partial_iz_o>0$; by
\cref{lem:gain}, $h_i=s_i\partial_iz_o>\eta/2$, so the minimum of $h_i$ on the compact set $\B$ is positive.
\emph{Error.} Since $\sig$ is $\tfrac14$-Lipschitz,
$\sup_\B|p_\theta-f|\le\tfrac14\bigl(\sup_\B|z_o-H|+\eta\,|\I|+\sup_\B|F_\delta-F|\bigr)<\varepsilon$ for $\eta$ and
$\delta$ small enough. Finite termination of \cref{alg:bb} follows from \cref{prop:term}.

\paragraph{Bounds}
(a) For $x\in Q$, $w_j^\top x+b_j=w_j^\top m+b_j+w_j^\top(x-m)$ with $|w_j^\top(x-m)|\le|w_j|^\top r$, attained at a
vertex, so $z_j(Q)=[\alpha_j,\beta_j]$. Since $\sig'$ is even and unimodal with its maximum at $0$, its minimum over an
interval is attained at an endpoint and its maximum at the point of the interval closest to $0$; summing
$c_j\sig'(z_j(x))\ge c_j\underline d_j$ ($c_j\ge0$) and $\ge c_j\overline d_j$ ($c_j<0$) gives the first term of
\eqref{eq:lbx}. (b) The range of $\sig''$ on $[\alpha_j,\beta_j]$ is attained at the endpoints or at $\pm t^\ast$, so
$[\underline e_j,\overline e_j]$ encloses $\sig''(z_j(x))$ for $x\in Q$, and interval arithmetic on
$\sum_jc_jW_{jd}\sig''(z_j)$ encloses $\partial_dh$ on $Q$. The mean value theorem gives
$h(x)\ge h(m)-\sum_d|x_d-m_d|\sup_Q|\partial_dh|\ge h(m)-\sum_dr_d\max\{|G^-_d|,|G^+_d|\}$. (c) If $G^-_d>0$, $h$ is
increasing in $x_d$ on $Q$, so $\min_Qh$ is attained on the face $x_d=l_d$ (symmetrically for $G^+_d<0$); replacing $Q$
by that face does not change $\min_Qh$. (d) $\varphi$ is a sum of functions of one coordinate each, so its minimum over a
product of intervals is the sum of the one-dimensional minima, given by the unimodality of $\sig'$ as in (a).

\paragraph{\Cref{thm:sound}}
Every point of $\B$ belongs to a box of the initial list, and every box is either split into two boxes that cover it,
reduced to a face on which $h$ attains its minimum over the box (c), or discarded. A box is discarded only if
$L(Q)\ge0$, which by (a)--(b) implies $h\ge0$ on $Q$; or, in pre-activation space, if $\min_R\varphi\ge0$, which implies
$h(x)=\varphi(Wx+b)\ge0$ for every $x$ with $Wx+b\in R$ (d); or if $R$ does not meet $Z$, in which case no $x\in\B$ maps
into $R$. If the search ends with an empty list, $\min_\B h_i\ge0$ and, by \cref{lem:gain}, $s_ig_i\ge0$ on $\B$. A
counterexample is returned only after evaluating $h(\hat x)<0$ at a point $\hat x\in\B$ (the centre of a sub-box of $\B$,
or the solution of a linear program with bounds $0\le x\le1$).

\paragraph{\Cref{prop:term}}
Inputs with $\sum_j|c_jW_{jd}|=0$ do not affect $h$ and are never split; along every other coordinate the splitting rule
halves a coordinate of largest weighted width, so under breadth-first processing the radius of every box at depth $k$
tends to zero with $k$. Let $\Gamma=\frac{\sqrt3}{18}\sum_d\sum_j|c_jW_{jd}|$, an upper bound of
$\max\{|G^-_d|,|G^+_d|\}$ summed over $d$. If $\min_\B h=\delta>0$, the mean-value term of \eqref{eq:lbx} is at least
$\delta-\Gamma\max_dr_d$, positive once $\max_dr_d<\delta/\Gamma$, so every branch is discarded at finite depth. If
$\min_\B h<0$, continuity gives a box $Q^\ast\subseteq\B$ on which $h<0$; boxes that contain a point of $Q^\ast$ are never
discarded, and at finite depth one of them lies inside $Q^\ast$, whose centre is then a counterexample. The monotonicity
test only replaces boxes by faces that contain their minimisers and does not affect either argument.

\section{Gradients of the gains}\label{app:gradients}
Write $a_j=\sig(z_j)$, $\sig_j'=a_j(1-a_j)$, $\sig_j''=\sig_j'(1-2a_j)$, $o=\sig(z_o)$, $\sig_o'=o(1-o)$,
$\sig_o''=\sig_o'(1-2o)$ and $u_i=\sum_jv_j\sig_j'W_{ji}$, so that $g_i=\sig_o'u_i$. Then, for $k=1,\dots,n$ and
$l=1,\dots,p$,
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial W_{kl}}&=\sig_o''\,v_k\sig_k'x_l\,u_i+\sig_o'\bigl(v_k\sig_k''x_lW_{ki}+v_k\sig_k'\,[i=l]\bigr),\\
\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial b_k}&=\sig_o''\,v_k\sig_k'\,u_i+\sig_o'\,v_k\sig_k''W_{ki},\\
\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial v_k}&=\sig_o''\,a_k\,u_i+\sig_o'\,\sig_k'W_{ki},\\
\frac{\partial g_i}{\partial c}&=\sig_o''\,u_i ,
\end{align*}
where $[i=l]$ is 1 if $i=l$ and 0 otherwise. These expressions are checked against finite differences in the test suite
of the code.
