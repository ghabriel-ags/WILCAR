\author[ufba]{Ghabriel A. G. de Sá\corref{cor}}

  city={Salvador}, state={BA}, country={Brazil}}

\begin{abstract}
The probability of the positive class is often known to increase or decrease with some inputs. We train constructive
single feedforward neural networks, grown one hidden unit at a time, to respect such gain signs on the whole input
domain and to prove it. Earlier work imposed the gain sign at a reference point; here it holds at every point. We show
that sign constraints on the weights, the classical way to guarantee monotonicity, cannot approximate simple monotone
targets with one hidden layer, whereas networks that are monotone on the domain are universal approximators. To find
them, gain-sign constraints are imposed at anchor points that grow with the counterexamples of an adversarial search,
and the final network is checked by a sound branch-and-bound certificate; if the check fails, a sign-constrained
solution is returned, so every network is certified. On seven synthetic targets with a known probability, the certified
networks have 30\% lower error than sign-constrained ones. On eight datasets, including the COMPAS, Heart Disease and
Loan Defaulter benchmarks, under nested cross-validation, they have the best average rank among eight methods, including constrained
monotonic networks and monotone gradient-boosted trees, and are certified in every fold, whereas only 35--41\% of the
unconstrained networks are monotone. The result is a smooth model with one to five hidden units, with gains that exist
everywhere and have the expected signs, where monotone trees need hundreds of piecewise-constant splits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In many classification problems the direction of the effect of some inputs is known in advance: the probability of
death after heart failure should not decrease as the ejection fraction falls, and the probability of default should not
decrease as the debt-to-income ratio grows. Properly validated networks may still contradict such gain signs
\citep{Sa2026}, and their predictions then cannot be defended to a clinician or a regulator. Networks that
enforce the known directions are \emph{partially monotonic} \citep{DanielsVelikova2010}. \emph{Soft} approaches
penalise violations at sampled points \citep{SillAbuMostafa1996} and give no guarantee elsewhere. \emph{Structural}
approaches are monotone by construction: non-negative weights \citep{ArcherWang1993}, min-max networks \citep{Sill1997},
lattices \citep{You2017}, constrained activations \citep{Runje2023,Sartor2025}, Lipschitz-bounded networks
\citep{Nolte2023} and monotone Kolmogorov--Arnold networks \citep{PoloMolina2026kan}; they fix a specialised
architecture in advance. \emph{Certified} approaches train an ordinary network and verify it, by mixed-integer
programming \citep{Liu2020}, SMT counterexamples added to the training data \citep{Sivaraman2020} or Lipschitz coverings
of the domain \citep{PoloMolina2026cert}.

Monotone constraints are also available in gradient-boosted trees \citep{ChenGuestrin2016}, which are strong on
tabular data; but a tree ensemble is piecewise constant, has no gains, is flat outside the training range and needs
hundreds of trees. When the model is to be read as an input--output relation with static gains, or embedded in
optimisation and control, a smooth network is needed. Single feedforward neural networks (SFNN), with one hidden layer,
are a natural choice for small and medium tabular data, and constructive algorithms define their number of hidden units by growing the hidden layer
\citep{Fahlman1990,KwokYeung1997,HuangChenSiew2006}. WILCAR, which initialises the weights by linearisation and reuses
them along the growth, was proposed for regression and multiclass classification \citep{Sa2022,Fontes2021}. In earlier
work we imposed gain signs on constructive SFNNs as hard constraints \citep{Sa2026}: the static gain of each input,
computed by finite differences at a reference operating point, must have the expected sign, and the constraint may be
repeated at several points when the gain can change across operating regions. Such constraints hold where they are
imposed. The natural way to impose them everywhere, non-negative (sign-constrained) weights, is not universal for
monotone functions with one hidden layer \citep{DanielsVelikova2010,Gupta2016}. This work closes the gap for binary
classification:
\begin{itemize}
\item single-hidden-layer sigmoid networks that are monotone on the input box, with a positive gain margin, are
universal approximators of continuous partially monotone functions (\cref{thm:ua}); the obstruction lies in the sign
parametrisation, not in the depth;
\item a counterexample-guided training imposes the gain signs at anchor points that grow with the violations found by
an adversarial search, and a repair-and-fallback step makes every returned network certified (\cref{sec:cegis});
\item a sound branch-and-bound certificate, specialised to one hidden layer, proves the gain signs on the whole domain
(\cref{sec:certificate}); unlike the mixed-integer certificate of \citet{Liu2020} it handles sigmoid units, and unlike
the Lipschitz covering of \citet{PoloMolina2026cert} its cost does not grow exponentially with the number of inputs;
\item synthetic targets with a known probability and eight real datasets, under nested cross-validation, compare the
approach with six baselines, including Min-Max, constrained monotonic and Lipschitz monotonic networks.
\end{itemize}
Proofs, derivations, settings and complete results are in the Supplementary Material.

\section{Gain signs and monotonicity}\label{sec:problem}

Inputs are min-max scaled on the training data, so that they lie in the box $\B=[0,1]^p$, and $y\in\{0,1\}$. An SFNN
with $n$ sigmoid hidden units models
\begin{equation}\label{eq:slfn}
p_\theta(x)=\sig\bigl(z_o(x)\bigr),\qquad z_o(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n} v_j\,\sig\bigl(z_j(x)\bigr)+c,\qquad z_j(x)=w_j^\top x+b_j ,
\end{equation}
with $\theta=(W,b,v,c)$, $W\in\R^{n\times p}$ with rows $w_j^\top$, and $\sig(t)=1/(1+e^{-t})$; $p_\theta(x)$ estimates
$P(Y=1\mid x)$, where $Y=1$ is the positive class (the event of interest). The expected gain signs form a vector
$s\in\{-1,0,+1\}^p$: $s_i=+1$ ($-1$) states that $P(Y=1\mid x)$ does not decrease (increase) with $x_i$, and $s_i=0$
leaves $x_i$ free; $\I=\{i:s_i\neq0\}$.

\begin{definition}[Partial monotonicity]\label{def:mono}
$f:\B\to\R$ is \emph{$s$-monotone on $\B$} if $s_i\bigl(f(x+te_i)-f(x)\bigr)\ge0$ for every $i\in\I$, $x\in\B$ and $t\ge0$
with $x+te_i\in\B$. For $p_\theta$ this holds if and only if the \emph{gains} $g_i=\partial p_\theta/\partial x_i$
satisfy $s_ig_i(x)\ge0$ for all $x\in\B$, $i\in\I$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Gains]\label{lem:gain}
$g_i(x)=\sig'(z_o(x))\sum_{j}v_j\sig'(z_j(x))W_{ji}$. With $c_{ij}=s_iv_jW_{ji}$ and
$h_i(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{ij}\,\sig'(z_j(x))$, $s_ig_i(x)=\sig'(z_o(x))\,h_i(x)$; since $\sig'>0$, $s_ig_i(x)\ge0\iff h_i(x)\ge0$.
\end{lemma}

The sign of the gain is therefore carried by $h_i$, a weighted sum of positive bell-shaped functions $\sig'(z_j)$ with
weights $c_{ij}$ of either sign. We compare four ways of constraining it, all imposed as hard constraints of the training
problem:
\begin{align}
\text{mean:}\quad & \frac{1}{|\A|}\sum_{a\in\A}s_i\,g_i(a)\ \ge\ \varepsilon_{m}, && i\in\I, \label{eq:mean}\\
\text{anchor:}\quad & s_i\,g_i(a)\ \ge\ \varepsilon_{p}, && i\in\I,\ a\in\A, \label{eq:point}\\
\text{sign:}\quad & c_{ij}=s_i\,v_j\,W_{ji}\ \ge\ 0, && i\in\I,\ j=1,\dots,n, \label{eq:sign}
\end{align}
and the counterexample-guided mode of \cref{sec:cegis}. $\A\subset\B$ is a finite set of points. The mean constraint,
over the training inputs, fixes the sign of the average gain, the global effect of each input \citep{HardleStoker1989}.
The anchor constraint fixes the gain sign at chosen points; with $\A$ the reference point it is the constraint of
\citet{Sa2026}, and here $\A$ holds ten $k$-means centres. Neither constrains the gain between the points. Monotonicity
on $\B$ implies both (with zero margins). The sign constraint holds everywhere:

\begin{proposition}[Sign constraints]\label{prop:sign}
If \eqref{eq:sign} holds, then $h_i\ge0$ on $\R^p$ and $p_\theta$ is $s$-monotone on $\R^p$.
\end{proposition}

This is the non-negative-weight construction \citep{ArcherWang1993,DanielsVelikova2010} for partial monotonicity:
every hidden unit must itself be monotone, with the expected sign, in every constrained input. That costs
expressiveness (\cref{fig:surfaces}). The target of \cref{fig:surfaces}, increasing in both inputs, has an L-shaped
decision boundary. Under \eqref{eq:sign} the logit is a sum of ridges increasing in both inputs, the boundary cuts the
corner (panel c), and the error is three times that of the unconstrained network, which forms the corner but decreases
in some input where the probability is flat and the data carry little information (panel b). The network of
\cref{sec:cegis} forms the corner and is certified (panel d): two of its units decrease in one input each (panel f) and
together act like the term $-|x_1-x_2|$ in $\min\{x_1,x_2\}=\tfrac12(x_1+x_2-|x_1-x_2|)$. With more units the
sign-constrained error stops decreasing, whereas the certified networks follow the unconstrained ones (Supplementary
\cref{S-fig:capacity}). The obstruction lies in the parametrisation:

\begin{theorem}[Universal approximation with a certificate margin]\label{thm:ua}
Let $f:\B\to(0,1)$ be continuous and $s$-monotone. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an SFNN \eqref{eq:slfn} with
$\sup_\B|p_\theta-f|<\varepsilon$ and $\min_{\B}h_i>0$ for every $i\in\I$.
\end{theorem}

The proof smooths $f$ by a monotone mollification, adds a small linear term that creates a gain margin, and uses the
density of SFNNs in $C^1$ \citep{Hornik1991}; it is elementary, and the statement is, to our knowledge, not found in the
literature on monotone networks, which concerns sign-constrained weights \citep{DanielsVelikova2010,Mikulincer2025,Sartor2025}. The margin also makes the certificate of \cref{sec:certificate} complete
for such networks (\cref{prop:term}).

\section{Certified constructive training}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Constructive networks}\label{sec:constructive}
A constructive method trains networks with $n=1,2,\dots$ hidden units and stops when a validation criterion has not
improved for a number of steps (the patience). \emph{WILCAR} \citep{Sa2022,Fontes2021} initialises the first unit by
linearising the network around an equilibrium point (the mean input) and approximating the optimal linear regressor,
and \emph{reuses weights}: the network with $n$ units starts from the one with $n-1$ units plus a new unit. \emph{RIXM}
re-initialises all weights at every size \citep{Glorot2010}; \emph{ELM} \citep{Huang2006} draws the hidden layer at random
and solves the output layer. Their constrained versions are WILCAR-C, RIXM-C and ELM-C. WILCAR and RIXM minimise
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss}
J(\theta)=-\frac1N\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigl[y_k\log p_\theta(x_k)+(1-y_k)\log\bigl(1-p_\theta(x_k)\bigr)\bigr]
+\frac{\lambda_0}{N}\bigl(\|W\|_F^2+\|v\|_2^2\bigr),
\end{equation}
the cross-entropy with a Gaussian prior of precision $\lambda_0$ on the weights, by L-BFGS \citep{LiuNocedal1989}; the
constrained versions start from that optimum and solve the constrained problem by SLSQP \citep{Kraft1994}, with exact
gradients of the gains (Supplementary \cref{S-sup:gradients}).

\subsection{Counterexample-guided constraints}\label{sec:cegis}
The \emph{cegis} mode (\cref{alg:cegis}), named after counterexample-guided inductive synthesis \citep{SolarLezama2006},
aims at the class of \cref{thm:ua}. At size $n$ the network is re-solved by SLSQP under the anchor constraints
\eqref{eq:point}. An adversarial search then minimises each $h_i$ over $\B$ (L-BFGS-B from 16 random points and 8
training points with the smallest $h_i$, with $\nabla_xh_i=\sum_jc_{ij}\sig''(z_j)w_j$); for each $i$, the minimiser
with $h_i<0$ becomes an anchor and the network is re-solved.
Anchors are kept from size to size. This is the exchange scheme of semi-infinite programming \citep{Hettich1993}: the
infinite set of constraints $h_i(x)\ge0$, $x\in\B$, is replaced by a finite set that grows with the most violated points.
After the final refit the certificate of \cref{sec:certificate} is run; its counterexamples also become anchors
(\emph{repair}). If the certificate is undecided or still fails after $R_{\mathrm{rep}}$ repairs, the weights with $c_{ij}<0$ are
set to zero and the network is re-solved under \eqref{eq:sign} (\emph{fallback}). COMET \citep{Sivaraman2020} also
trains with counterexamples, but as labelled data of a soft penalty and with monotone envelopes at prediction time; here
they are hard constraints and the returned network itself is monotone.

\begin{proposition}[Certified output]\label{prop:always}
The network returned by the cegis mode is $s$-monotone on $\B$: it is either certified (\cref{thm:sound}) or satisfies
\eqref{eq:sign} (\cref{prop:sign}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{algorithm*}[!tbp]
\caption{Constructive step $n$ in the cegis mode.}\label{alg:cegis}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require data $(X,y)$, gain signs $s$, anchors $\A$ ($k$-means centres and earlier counterexamples), initial weights
$\theta^{(0)}$ (WILCAR: previous network plus one unit; RIXM: random)
\State $\theta\gets$ L-BFGS on $J$ from $\theta^{(0)}$; \ $\theta\gets$ SLSQP on $J$ subject to \eqref{eq:point} on $\A$, from $\theta$
\For{$r=1,\dots,R_{\mathrm{cex}}$}
  \State $C\gets\{\hat x_i: i\in\I,\ h_i(\hat x_i)<0\}$, \ $\hat x_i\gets$ multi-start L-BFGS-B on $\min_{x\in\B}h_i(x)$
  \If{$C=\emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
  \State $\A\gets\A\cup C$; \ $\theta\gets$ SLSQP on the enlarged $\A$, warm-started at $\theta$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\theta$, $\A$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

\subsection{A sound certificate}\label{sec:certificate}
By \cref{lem:gain}, $p_\theta$ is $s$-monotone on $\B$ if and only if $\min_\B h_i\ge0$ for every $i\in\I$. A branch and
bound decides this for each $i$ (Supplementary \cref{S-alg:bb}): it bounds $h=h_i$ from below on a box; if the bound is
non-negative the whole box is proved; if $h<0$ at a point of the box, that point is a counterexample; otherwise the box
is split. On a box $Q=[l,u]$ with centre $m$ and radius $r$, each pre-activation ranges exactly over
$[\alpha_j,\beta_j]$, with $\alpha_j,\beta_j=w_j^\top m+b_j\mp|w_j|^\top r$; since $\sig'$ is even and unimodal, its
minimum there is $\underline{d}_j=\min\{\sig'(\alpha_j),\sig'(\beta_j)\}$ and its maximum
$\overline{d}_j=\sig'(\clip(0;\alpha_j,\beta_j))$. With an interval enclosure $[G^-_d,G^+_d]$ of $\partial h/\partial x_d$
on $Q$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lbx}
\min_{x\in Q}h(x)\ \ge\ \max\Bigl\{\sum_{c_j\ge0}c_j\underline{d}_j+\sum_{c_j<0}c_j\overline{d}_j,\ \
h(m)-\sum_{d=1}^{p}r_d\max\{|G^-_d|,|G^+_d|\}\Bigr\},
\end{equation}
the natural interval extension and the mean-value form \citep{Moore2009}; the monotonicity test of interval global
optimisation \citep{HansenWalster2004} collapses a box onto a face when $G^-_d>0$ or $G^+_d<0$. When $n<p$ and the input-space search
exhausts a quarter of its box budget, the search continues in pre-activation space, where $h=\varphi(Wx+b)$ with $\varphi(z)=\sum_jc_j\sig'(z_j)$ separable, so that its minimum on a box
$R=[\zeta^l,\zeta^u]$ is computed exactly:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lbz}
\min_{z\in R}\varphi(z)=\sum_{c_j\ge0}c_j\min\{\sig'(\zeta^l_j),\sig'(\zeta^u_j)\}+\sum_{c_j<0}c_j\,\sig'\bigl(\clip(0;\zeta^l_j,\zeta^u_j)\bigr);
\end{equation}
boxes outside the zonotope $\{Wx+b:x\in\B\}$ are discarded by a linear program in $x$.

\begin{theorem}[Soundness]\label{thm:sound}
If the branch and bound returns \textsc{certified} for every $i\in\I$, $p_\theta$ is $s$-monotone on $\B$; a returned
counterexample $\hat x$ lies in $\B$ and satisfies $s_ig_i(\hat x)<0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proposition}[Termination]\label{prop:term}
If $\min_\B h_i\neq0$, the input-space search with an unlimited box budget terminates after finitely many boxes.
\end{proposition}

Bounding a box costs $O(np)$ operations. The number of boxes is exponential in $\min\{n,p\}$ in the worst case, but in
practice it is governed by the margin $\min_\B h_i$. Undecided cases (a minimum exactly at zero, or the box budget) go to
the fallback. The certificate applies to any SFNN, so it also measures how often unconstrained and mean- or
anchor-constrained networks are monotone.

\subsection{Dynamic cross-validation}\label{sec:dcv}
Dynamic cross-validation (DCV) selects the number of hidden units inside a training set: $K$ stratified folds, one model
per fold, all grown together; at size $n$ the criterion is the sum of the $K$ validation cross-entropies, and growth
stops after the patience. The final network is retrained on the whole training set with the selected size
(Supplementary \cref{S-alg:dcv}). The criterion needs validation curves that change smoothly with $n$, which weight
reuse favours; DCV is therefore used with WILCAR, which is also run with a stratified 80/20 hold-out split and the
validation MCC, the selection used by the other methods.

\section{Case studies and experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

\emph{Synthetic targets.} Seven $s$-monotone probabilities $P(Y=1\mid x)$ on $[0,1]^p$, $p\in\{2,3\}$ (Supplementary
\cref{S-tab:synthdef}): the ``and'' target of \cref{fig:surfaces}, an ``or'' (maximum), a product, a three-input
minimum, versions with a free periodic input and with a decreasing input, and a smooth ``wave'' without min/max
structure; 2000 uniform inputs with Bernoulli labels each. The error against the known probability is measured on
$2\times10^4$ uniform points. Since the question is what each class of networks can represent, every constructive step
keeps the best of three initialisations on these targets.

\emph{Real data.} Eight datasets (\cref{tab:datasets}): five from the UCI repository, with gain signs from domain
knowledge (Supplementary \cref{S-tab:signals}), and the three benchmarks of the monotonic-network literature, with the
preprocessing and monotone features of \citet{Runje2023}.

\IfFileExists{tables/datasets.tex}{\input{tables/datasets.tex}}{}

\emph{Protocol.} Nested cross-validation \citep{VarmaSimon2006,CawleyTalbot2010}: an outer stratified 5-fold split
repeated five times (twice for Loan Defaulter); scaling, number of hidden units (or hyper-parameters) and refit use
only the outer training set. All methods share the splits. The three benchmarks are also run on their official
train/test splits with five seeds (\cref{sec:lit}).

\emph{Methods.} WILCAR, RIXM and ELM, unconstrained and with the four constraints of \cref{sec:problem}
($\varepsilon_m=0.05$, $\varepsilon_p=10^{-3}$, $\lambda_0=0.3$); logistic regression, free and with sign-bounded
coefficients (LR, LR-C); XGBoost \citep{ChenGuestrin2016} and LightGBM \citep{Ke2017}, free and with monotone
constraints; a one-hidden-layer MLP \citep{Pedregosa2011}; and three networks monotone by construction, Min-Max
\citep{Sill1997}, constrained monotonic neural networks (CMNN, \citealp{Runje2023}) and Lipschitz monotonic networks
(LMN, \citealp{Nolte2023}), from their authors' packages. Settings and grids are in Supplementary
\cref{S-tab:settings,S-tab:hyper}; each selection loop has a budget of one hour.

\emph{Metrics.} Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC, \citealp{ChiccoJurman2020}); AUC and Brier score in the
Supplementary Material; on synthetic data, the mean absolute error against the true probability. Gain-sign conformity is
measured on the whole domain: the share of (point, constrained input) pairs with a wrong-sign gain on $10^4$ uniform
points of $\B$, and the outcome of the certificate. Methods are compared with the Friedman test, the Iman--Davenport
correction and Holm's procedure \citep{Demsar2006}, and pairwise with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test over outer folds.

\section{Results and discussion}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Synthetic targets}\label{sec:synth}
\IfFileExists{tables/synthetic.tex}{\input{tables/synthetic.tex}}{}
The certified networks have lower error than the sign-constrained ones on every target, 30\% lower on average (0.053
against 0.076), and are certified in all 175 runs (\cref{tab:synth}). Unconstrained networks are more accurate (0.044)
but monotone in only 35\% of the runs. The mean constraint changes little, because on these targets the average gain
already has the expected sign. Among the monotone models, Min-Max, LMN and gradient-boosted trees reach 0.041--0.044:
Min-Max represents the minimum and maximum targets exactly, and the SFNNs lose most on ``or'' and on the three-input
minimum, whose sharp corners a sum of sigmoid ridges approximates slowly.

\subsection{Real data}\label{sec:perf}
\IfFileExists{tables/main_mcc.tex}{\input{tables/main_mcc.tex}}{}
\IfFileExists{tables/modes.tex}{\input{tables/modes.tex}}{}
The Friedman test rejects equal performance over the eight methods of \cref{tab:main_mcc} (Iman--Davenport
$p=0.006$). The counterexample-guided WILCAR has the best average rank (2.9), followed by CMNN (3.3), monotone XGBoost
and the unconstrained WILCAR (3.5); after Holm's correction only Min-Max is significantly worse (adjusted $p=0.02$).
All constrained SFNNs, including RIXM, are in Supplementary \cref{S-tab:main_mcc_full}. Over the 185 outer folds,
certified WILCAR differs from the unconstrained one by $+0.002$ MCC (Wilcoxon $p=0.07$, with ties split) and from CMNN by $-0.004$
($p=0.20$), and exceeds LMN by $0.017$ and Min-Max by $0.019$ ($p<10^{-4}$). AUC and Brier score give the same
picture (Supplementary \cref{S-tab:main_auc,S-tab:main_brier}): mean AUC 0.877 for certified WILCAR, unconstrained WILCAR
and CMNN, 0.873 for LMN, 0.869 for Min-Max and 0.881 for the monotone tree ensembles. Monotone XGBoost is ahead on ALG and HF, the networks on HD (0.589 against 0.515), and the
certified SFNN is ahead of the trees on five of the eight datasets.

Only 35\% of the unconstrained networks are monotone on the domain, with 11\% of wrong-sign pairs (\cref{tab:modes}).
The mean constraint raises this to 75\% and the anchor constraint, which leaves the space between anchors free, to
64\%; neither gives a guarantee. The sign and cegis constraints are monotone in every fold, at no cost in MCC. Over the 555 cegis runs of WILCAR and RIXM, 97.3\% of the networks were certified by the branch and bound and 2.7\%
(15, 14 of them on Loan Defaulter) were returned by the fallback.
The median cost per outer fold is 22 s for certified WILCAR and 18 s for unconstrained WILCAR; the mean is dominated by BCD
(27 constrained inputs) and Loan Defaulter, where 22 of the 555 selection loops reached the one-hour budget. On a separate
set of 168 networks, the certificate decided 96\% of those produced by a cegis step, whose margins are thin by design,
with a median time below one millisecond (Supplementary \cref{S-tab:certbench}).

On real data the sign and cegis constraints give the same accuracy. Selection favours small networks (median of 5
hidden units for certified WILCAR with DCV and 1 with the hold-out split; Supplementary \cref{S-tab:size}), and with a
single unit the two constraints coincide. The flexibility of cegis matters when the target needs more units, as in
\cref{tab:synth}.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
The constraints form a hierarchy: the mean constraint fixes the sign of the global effect of each input, the anchor
constraint and the reference-point constraint of our earlier work fix it at chosen points, and the sign and cegis
constraints fix it everywhere. The weaker constraints often yield monotone networks, but only the certificate can tell
which. Sign constraints give the guarantee through the parameters, at the price shown in \cref{fig:surfaces} and
\cref{tab:synth}; the cegis mode gives it through verification and keeps the flexibility of \cref{thm:ua}. Growth suits the
scheme: anchors found at size $n$ remain valid at size $n+1$, and small networks are cheap to certify, because the
pre-activation search works in dimension $n$.

\emph{Against monotone trees.} Gradient-boosted trees with monotone splits are the strongest baseline and are monotone
by construction; they tie with the certified SFNNs in MCC and AUC. The difference is the model. A tree ensemble is a
piecewise-constant function: its gains are zero or undefined, it is flat outside the training range, and it takes
100--300 trees of depth 2--4 where the certified SFNN uses one to five sigmoid units. The SFNN is a single smooth
equation with gains defined at every point, certified to have the expected signs on the whole box; it can be
differentiated, embedded in an optimiser or a model predictive controller, and read as the static-gain model of
\citet{Sa2026}. The guarantee of the trees is a property of the splits; the guarantee here is a property of the
function.

The certificate relies on the separability of one hidden layer in pre-activation space; deeper networks would need
bound propagation \citep{Gowal2019,Liu2021verif}. The guarantee holds on the training box $\B$; the certificate is sound up to
floating-point rounding and the feasibility tolerance of the linear programs, which directed rounding would remove. The quadratic programs limit constrained networks to tens of hidden units and
training sets to tens of thousands of rows.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
Single-hidden-layer networks that are monotone on their domain are universal approximators of continuous partially
monotone functions, while sign-constrained ones are not. Counterexample-guided constraints, a sound certificate and a
sign-constrained fallback reach that class with constructive networks. Every network returned is certified; on
synthetic targets the certified networks have 30\% lower error than sign-constrained ones, and on real data they have the best
average rank among eight methods, tied with constrained monotonic networks and monotone gradient-boosted trees, with a
smooth model of one to five hidden units in place of hundreds of piecewise-constant trees. Extending the certificate to deeper
constructive networks is a natural next step.
